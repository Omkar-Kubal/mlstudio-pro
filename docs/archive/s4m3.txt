Clustering
Clustering is an unsupervised learning task that seeks to organize a set of objects into groups (clusters) such that similar objects end up in the same cluster and dissimilar objects end up in different clusters
. In other words, we wish to find a partition of the data where intra-cluster similarity is high and inter-cluster similarity is low
. Clustering is a key technique in exploratory data analysis and finds applications in pattern recognition, information retrieval, bioinformatics, and many other fields
. Unlike supervised learning, clustering does not use labeled examples – the algorithm discovers the grouping structure directly from the feature patterns
. Key points:
Clustering groups data points so that those in the same cluster are “similar” to each other, while those in different clusters are “dissimilar”
.
There is no single definition of “cluster”; different algorithms capture different notions (e.g. compact vs. connected vs. dense)
.
Clustering is inherently an exploratory, iterative process – one often adjusts the method or parameters until the clusters make sense for the data
.

K-Means Intuition
K-means is a centroid-based clustering algorithm that is easy to understand and widely used
. It partitions 
n
n points into 
k
k clusters by iteratively improving cluster centers (centroids) to minimize within-cluster variance. Intuitively, k-means works as follows:
Initialization: Randomly choose 
k
k initial centroids (or use a smart initializer like k-means++).
Assignment Step: Assign each data point to the nearest centroid (using, e.g., Euclidean distance)
.
Update Step: Recompute each centroid as the mean of the points assigned to it.
Repeat: Alternate between assignment and update until cluster assignments stop changing or a maximum number of iterations is reached
.
K-means seeks to minimize the within-cluster sum of squared distances (WCSS) to the centroids
. In practice, the algorithm converges quickly but may only find a local optimum of this objective
. The result is 
k
k “round” clusters, each described by its centroid
. Because k-means uses the mean, it is sensitive to outliers and assumes roughly spherical clusters of similar size. Key points:
K-means clusters data around centroids by minimizing squared Euclidean distance
.
Each iteration assigns points to the nearest centroid and then recomputes the centroids (mean of cluster points)
.
K-means converges to a (possibly local) optimum quickly, but the choice of initial centroids can affect the result.
It requires specifying 
k
k in advance and implicitly assumes clusters are convex (spherical in Euclidean space).
Visual/Animation Explanation:
Iterative steps: Animate the k-means loop: show initial random centroids, then color points by assignment. Then show centroids moving to the mean of each cluster, then points reassigning, etc. Continue until convergence. Framer Motion can smoothly animate points “jumping” between clusters and centroids sliding to new positions.

Example Python: Apply K-Means on a 2D blob dataset and output the learned centroids.

from sklearn.cluster import KMeans

X, _ = make_blobs(n_samples=100, centers=3, random_state=0)

kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=3, random_state=0)
kmeans.fit(X)
print("Centroids:\n", kmeans.cluster_centers_)

Choosing k
A practical challenge with k-means (and other centroid-based methods) is deciding the number of clusters 
k
k. There is no one-size-fits-all rule for choosing 
k
k; instead, we rely on heuristics and validation methods. Common techniques include:
Elbow Method: Plot the sum of squared distances (inertia) within clusters versus 
k
k. The inertia will decrease as 
k
k increases, but often there is a “knee” or elbow where the rate of decrease sharply changes
. Choosing 
k
k near this elbow gives a balance between fitting the data well and keeping 
k
k small
.
Silhouette Score: For each 
k
k, compute the silhouette coefficient for the clustering. The silhouette score measures how well each point lies within its cluster (cohesion) compared to other clusters (separation)
. Values range from –1 to +1, with higher values indicating more distinct, well-separated clusters. We look for a high average silhouette score to pick 
k
k.
Gap Statistic: Compare the cluster dispersion to that expected under a null (random) distribution. The optimal 
k
k maximizes the gap between observed and expected dispersion
.
Information Criteria and Indices: Measures like the Davies–Bouldin index (DBI) and Calinski-Harabasz index evaluate intra-cluster compactness vs. inter-cluster separation
. For example, DBI is lower for better clusters
, while Calinski-Harabasz is higher for better clustering
.
External Validation: If some ground truth labels are available, one can use the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) or other label-based metrics to see which 
k
k best recovers the known clusters
.
In practice, one would try multiple values of 
k
k, look for a consistent “elbow” or peak silhouette, and also consider domain knowledge. No method is foolproof, so these heuristics guide but do not guarantee the perfect 
k
k. Visual/Animation Explanation:
Elbow Plot: Draw a curve of inertia vs

plt.ylabel('Inertia (sum of squared distances)')

Hierarchical Clustering
Hierarchical clustering builds a tree of clusters (a dendrogram) rather than a single flat partition
. There are two main flavors:
Agglomerative (Bottom-Up): Start with each point as its own cluster. Iteratively merge the two closest clusters (according to some distance/linkage criterion) until all points form one big cluster
. This produces a binary tree of merges. Agglomerative methods are the most common and are conceptually simple
.
Divisive (Top-Down): Start with all points in one cluster, and iteratively split clusters (typically the one with largest variance) into two until each point is separate or some stopping criterion is met
. Divisive approaches are less common in practice.
A linkage criterion determines how to measure the distance between clusters when merging. Common choices include: single-linkage (minimum distance between points of two clusters), complete-linkage (maximum distance), average-linkage (average pairwise distance), and Ward’s method (minimize increase in total variance)
. The choice of linkage heavily influences the shape of clusters (e.g. single-link tends to form long “chains,” while complete-link yields tighter blobs)
. The result of hierarchical clustering is typically visualized as a dendrogram: cutting the dendrogram at a chosen height yields a flat clustering
. Key points:
Hierarchical clustering does not require specifying 
k
k upfront (you can choose the level to cut the dendrogram)
.
It uses any distance metric (just a distance matrix) and a linkage rule to decide merges
.
Agglomerative (merge) is greedy and usually

. Divisive is often

Hierarchical models a cluster’s “connectivity”: it can capture nested clusters and is visualizable, but can be sensitive to noise and is computationally intensive for large

DBSCAN
DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) is a popular density-based algorithm

A core point has at least minPts points (including itself) within distance ε (i.e. a dense region)

A point q is directly reachable from a core point p if q is within ε of p. Only core points can directly reach others

Density vs. Distance Clustering
Clustering algorithms can be broadly categorized by their underlying model of a “cluster.” Distance-based methods (like k-means and hierarchical) rely on distance or connectivity: clusters are groups of points close by some metric or linked by a chain of nearest neighbors. Density-based methods (like DBSCAN) define clusters as dense regions separated by sparser areas
. In a density model, a cluster is a region in which every point has at least minPts neighbors within radius ε; points outside these regions are noise
. In contrast, a centroid-based model represents each cluster by its mean vector
, and a distance-connectivity model merges points that are close in the feature space. For example, DBSCAN (density-based) can find clusters of arbitrary shape and naturally handle outliers, while k-means (centroid-based) will always partition points into 
k
k roughly spherical clusters
. The key difference is that density clustering seeks contiguous high-density groups, whereas distance/centroid clustering (and also connectivity in hierarchical methods) forms clusters by proximity to a center or a chain of points. Key points:
Density-based (e.g. DBSCAN): clusters = connected dense regions; explicitly identifies noise
. Good for irregular shapes.
Distance/centroid-based (e.g. K-Means, Agglomerative): clusters = points near a center or linked by minimal distances
. Usually yield compact, convex clusters.
In practice, one often applies both: for example, k-means vs. DBSCAN on the same data to see which model fits better.

X, _ = make_moons(n_samples=150, noise=0.05, random_state=42)
km = KMeans(n_clusters=2, random_state=42).fit(X)
db = DBSCAN(eps=0.2, min_samples=5).fit(X)
print("K-Means labels:", km.labels_)
print("DBSCAN labels:", db.labels_)

Clustering Evaluation
Evaluating clustering quality is challenging due to lack of ground truth. We use two types of metrics: internal (intrinsic) and external (extrinsic). Internal metrics use only the data and cluster labels themselves, while external metrics compare to ground-truth labels if available. Common evaluation measures include:
Sum of Squared Errors (Inertia): This is the k-means objective (WCSS). Lower inertia means tighter clusters, but it always decreases as 
k
k grows, so it’s mainly used in methods like the elbow plot.
Silhouette Coefficient: For each point, silhouette = (b – a) / max(a, b), where a is average distance to points in its own cluster (cohesion) and b is average distance to points in the nearest other cluster (separation)
. The silhouette ranges from –1 to +1: a high value (close to +1) means the point is well clustered, while a low or negative value indicates a possible misclassification
. The mean silhouette score over all points (0 to 1) is often used: higher is better. A clustering with an average silhouette > 0.5 is generally considered reasonable
. Silhouette is best when clusters are convex.
Davies–Bouldin Index (DBI): An internal metric that measures average similarity between each cluster and its most similar (dissimilar) cluster
. It is defined so that lower values are better (closer clusters with smaller intracluster spread). DBI evaluates both compactness and separation
. Recent studies suggest DBI (with silhouette) can effectively assess convex clusters
.
Calinski-Harabasz Index: Ratio of between-cluster dispersion to within-cluster dispersion. Higher values imply better clustering (tight, well-separated clusters)
.
Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) & Mutual Information: If ground truth labels are known, ARI measures the agreement between the true labels and the clustering, adjusting for chance. ARI = 1 for perfect match, and near 0 for random assignment
. (The raw Rand index is the fraction of point pairs on which the two clusterings agree, but ARI corrects for expected chance agreement
.) Mutual information-based scores and purity are other external metrics.
In summary, one typically reports a combination of metrics. For unsupervised tasks without true labels, internal metrics like silhouette and DBI are used to compare different algorithms or parameter choices. If true labels exist (e.g. in a simulation), external scores like ARI quantify how well clustering recovered the known structure
. Visual/Animation Explanation:
Silhouette plot: Show the silhouette bars for each point (colored by cluster). Animate what happens when clustering quality improves (bars get longer) or worsens (more negative bars).
Metric dashboards: Create a dynamic “scoreboard” that updates silhouette, DBI, and ARI values as the clustering changes (e.g. when changing 
k
k or algorithm). Smoothly animate the numeric value transitions.
Cluster boundary effect: Animate a single outlier moving slowly away from its cluster and plot how its silhouette value decreases, visualizing the link between point position and score.
Example Python: Compute silhouette score, Davies–Bouldin index, and ARI for a clustering.
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score, davies_bouldin_score, adjusted_rand_score

X, y = make_blobs(n_samples=150, centers=3, random_state=42)
labels = KMeans(n_clusters=3, random_state=42).fit_predict(X)

sil_score = silhouette_score(X, labels)
db_index = davies_bouldin_score(X, labels)
ari = adjusted_rand_score(y, labels)  # assuming true labels y are known

print(f"Silhouette: {sil_score:.3f}")
print(f"Davies-Bouldin: {db_index:.3f}")
print(f"Adjusted Rand Index: {ari:.3f}")
